The 6 Turkish cases

As in German or Russian, Turkish uses grammatical cases, but they are formed with regular suffixes that harmonise with the last vowel of the word.

The Turkish cases with « ev » (house)
CaseSuffixFunctionExample
Nominative— (none)Subjectev (the house)
Accusative-(y)ı/i/u/üDefinite direct objectevi (the house, as object)
Dative-(y)a/eDirection, destinationeve (to the house)
Locative-da/dePlace (in, at)evde (at home)
Ablative-dan/denOrigin (from, since)evden (from the house)
Genitive-(n)ın/in/un/ünPossessionevin (of the house)
Key point
The suffix follows vowel harmony. And after a voiceless consonant (p, ç, t, k, s, ş, f, h), d becomes t: sokak → sokakta, et → ette. The grammar guides cover each case in detail.

Word order: SOV

Turkish follows Subject – Object – Verb order (SOV), unlike English (SVO). The verb goes at the end of the sentence.

English (SVO) versus Turkish (SOV)
English (SVO)Turkish (SOV)Word for word
I eat an apple.Ben bir elma yiyorum.I an apple eat.
Ali reads a book.Ali bir kitap okuyor.Ali a book reads.
Habit to build
At first, you will want to put the verb in the middle, as in English. Train yourself to put it at the end every time: that is the key to sounding natural.

Turkish conjugation

Conjugation is regular. Here is the present continuous (-yor) of the verb gelmek (to come):

gelmek — present continuous
PronounConjugationTranslation
BengeliyorumI am coming
SengeliyorsunYou are coming
OgeliyorHe / she is coming
BizgeliyoruzWe are coming
SizgeliyorsunuzYou are coming
OnlargeliyorlarThey are coming
Three special features
No grammatical gender: the pronoun o means he, she and even it.
No definite article: context and the definite accusative play that role.
The pronoun is often dropped: the verb ending is enough to show the person.

Method

Tips for learning effectively

Six habits that make a difference, especially at the start.

1

Master vowel harmony from the start

It is the backbone of the whole language. Once you have absorbed this concept, suffixes become intuitive and you will avoid the most common beginner mistakes.

2

Think in “suffix blocks”

Turkish is built like Lego: root + suffix + suffix. Rather than memorising whole sentences, learn the most common suffixes (-lar/-ler, -da/-de, -dan/-den, -a/-e) and combine them.

3

Get used to SOV order

At first, you will instinctively put the verb in the middle of the sentence. Train yourself to put it at the end every time: that is the key to sounding natural.

4

Watch Turkish series (dizi)

Turkey is the world's second-largest exporter of TV series after the United States. Dizi are an excellent way to immerse yourself in the language and culture. Start with French subtitles, then switch to Turkish subtitles.

5

Learn words of French origin

Turkish borrowed many words from French: kuaför (hairdresser), asansör (lift), tuvalet (toilet), pantolon (trousers), şoför (driver), gardırop (wardrobe), plaj (beach). Recognising these words gives you free starting vocabulary.

6

Don't neglect ı and ğ

The dotless ı (a guttural sound between « eu » and « i ») and the silent ğ are the two hardest sounds for a French speaker. Listen to native speakers and copy them from the start.

The 7 traps for French speakers

Turkish is written as it is pronounced, but some letters are not read as in French. Click the words to hear the correct pronunciation.

Letters that mislead French speakers
TrapListenThe correction
u = « ou »
su (water)
uzun (long)
 Do not say the « u » of « lune »: say « sou » and « ou-zoun ». It is the French « ou ».
ü = French « u »
güzel (beautiful)
üç (three)
 It is the « u » of « lune », with rounded lips. Compare tur (tour) and tür (kind, species).
ı dotless
kır (countryside)
sıcak (hot)
 A dark sound between « e » and « i »: tongue pulled back, lips spread. Not the French « i ».
ö = « eu »
göz (eye)
öğrenci (pupil)
 Rounded lips as for « o », but say « eu »: it is the « eu » of « peur ».
ğ silent
dağ (mountain)
değil (no, not)
 Never pronounce a « g »: dağ → « daa », değil → « déyil ». It lengthens the preceding vowel.
r tapped
bir (one)
Türkiye (Turkey)
 A light r: the tongue taps once behind the teeth, like the Spanish r in « pero ». Not the throaty French r.
Pure vowels
on (ten)
sonra (after)
 No nasalisation: « one », « son-ra ». Do not link words together as in French.
Stress
Stress most often falls on the last syllable, but not always: place names and some suffixes escape the rule. Don't guess: listen and copy.

8 grammar habits that prevent 80% of mistakes

Habits to make automatic
HabitExampleWhy
No definite articleBir kitap istiyorum. / Kitabı istiyorum.“I want a book” / “I want the book”. Definite articles are not translated: the accusative marks the definite direct object.
The verb at the end (SOV)Ben çay içiyorum.“I drink tea”, literally “I tea drink”. The verb closes the sentence.
“To have” = var / yokBir kedim var. / Kedim yok.“I have a cat” / “I don't have a cat”. Literally: “my cat exists / does not exist”.
Age with the locativeYirmi yaşındayım.“I am 20 years old”, literally “I am at my age of twenty”.
No plural after a numberüç kitap“Three books”, not üç kitaplar: the number is enough to mark the plural.
Suffixes in a fixed orderevlerimizden“From our houses”: ev + -ler + -imiz + -den. You stack them, you don't mix them.
The subject pronoun is optionalGeliyorum.“I am coming”: the ending -um is enough. Adding ben emphasises “me”.
Questions with a separate miGeliyor musun?“Are you coming?”: mi is written separately and harmonises (mi/mı/mu/mü).
Quick test
Arkadaşlarımızla İstanbul'da buluşuyoruz. — “We meet our friends in Istanbul.”
Break it down: arkadaş-lar-ımız-la (with our friends) + İstanbul-da (in Istanbul) + buluş-uyor-uz (we meet).

Culture

8 customs to know before a stay in Turkey

Language and politeness go together. These habits will open more doors than perfect vocabulary.

The customs that matter
SituationDoAvoid
Someone offers you teaAccept and thank them. If you really can't, put your hand on your heart.Refusing bluntly or leaving the glass full.
You enter someone's home or a mosqueTake off your shoes at the entrance.Keeping your shoes on.
You visit a mosqueCover your shoulders and legs (and your hair if you are a woman), keep a low profile and avoid prayer time.Shorts, low necklines, camera flash during prayer.
You are invited to a mealTaste everything, say Afiyet olsun before eating and Elinize sağlık to the cook.Refusing every dish or leaving without a word.
You shop at the bazaarAsk the price and bargain with a smile.Bargaining in a supermarket or shopping centre.
You pay at a restaurantLeave about 10% in cash if the service is good; check the bill.Assuming that service is always included.
You meet someoneShake hands, say Merhaba or Selam. To Hoş geldiniz, reply Hoş bulduk.Addressing an elder informally straight away: use siz.
You travel during RamadanKeep a low profile and avoid eating or drinking in public during the day in conservative neighbourhoods.Pointing out that you are not fasting.
The all-purpose word
Buyurun is used to invite: “come in”, “sit down”, “help yourself”, “please”. It is the Swiss Army knife of Turkish politeness.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions about learning Turkish

Is Turkish difficult to learn for a French speaker?

Turkish is classified in Category III by the American Foreign Service Institute, at the same level as Russian or Greek (about 1,100 hours of study). The main difficulties are vowel harmony, word order (SOV) and the agglutination system. On the other hand, Turkish uses the Latin alphabet, has no grammatical gender, has almost no exceptions to its rules and is pronounced exactly as it is written — considerable advantages over many other languages.

How long does it take to learn Turkish?

With 30 to 60 minutes of daily practice, allow about 3 months to master the alphabet, the grammar basics and the essential phrases, 6 to 12 months to reach an intermediate level that lets you hold a conversation, and 2 to 3 years for an advanced level.

Are Turkish and Arabic related?

No. Turkish is a Turkic language (Altaic family), while Arabic is a Semitic language — they are not related at all. However, Turkish borrowed many words from Arabic and Persian over the centuries, just as French borrowed from Latin and Greek. Since the 1928 language reform, Turkish has been written in the Latin alphabet and many of these borrowings have gradually been replaced by Turkish words.

Which languages are close to Turkish?

Turkish is close to Azerbaijani (partial mutual intelligibility), Turkmen, Uzbek and Kazakh. Learning Turkish makes learning these Central Asian languages much easier. There are also structural similarities (agglutination, vowel harmony, SOV order) with Japanese, Korean and Finnish, although the vocabulary is very different.

Is Turkish useful professionally?

Turkey is the world's 17th largest economy and a major trading partner of France. Speaking Turkish is an asset in international trade, tourism, diplomacy and the textile, construction and food industries. Fluency in Turkish is still rare among French-speaking professionals, which makes it a real distinguishing advantage in the job market.
